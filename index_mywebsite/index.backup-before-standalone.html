Reading the pattern: incident counts hover between 2,600–3,700 per year with no strong long-term trend, but every single year shows the same shape — a slow Q1 (cold, low cycling volume), a ramp through Q2, a Q3 peak, and a still-elevated Q4. This is consistent with bicycle theft tracking bicycle usage: more bikes parked outside in warm months means more targets. The dip in 2021–2023 loosely coincides with reduced downtown commuter traffic during and after pandemic-era office closures, though this dataset alone cannot confirm causation.

2 Spatial Autocorrelation

Moran's I = 0.288 — what the number actually means

The deck states theft risk is "spatially structured, not random" and cites Moran's I = 0.288 (p < 0.001). This scatter plot is the visual evidence behind that number.

How to read this chart

The x-axis is each neighborhood's theft count, standardized (mean-centered, scaled by standard deviation). The y-axis is the spatial lag — the average standardized count of its neighbors within a 5.6 km radius. If theft risk were randomly scattered across the city, points would form a flat, structureless cloud. Instead they trend upward: neighborhoods with high theft counts tend to sit next to other high-count neighborhoods, and low sits next to low.

The slope of that trend is Moran's I. A value of 0.288 (compared to an expected value near 0 under randomness) with p < 0.001 from a 999-permutation test means this clustering is statistically significant — it is very unlikely to have arisen by chance.

This is the formal justification for the whole project: if risk were spatially random, no interpolation method (kriging, IDW, or otherwise) could out-predict a simple citywide average. The clustering is what makes spatial prediction possible at all.

3 Method Detail

Inverse Distance Weighting, from formula to cross-validation

The deck names the IDW formula in passing. Here it is broken down term by term, alongside the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) procedure used to pick θ and to score every model in the comparison table below.

Ẑ(x0) = Σi=1n wi Z(xi) Σi=1n wi , wi = 1 diθ
Ẑ(x₀)Predicted log-theft-count at an unobserved location x₀.
Z(xᵢ)Observed log-theft-count at known neighborhood centroid i.
dᵢEuclidean distance (km, UTM projection) from x₀ to centroid i.
θPower parameter — how sharply influence decays with distance. Higher θ = more local, lower θ = smoother.
wᵢWeight assigned to neighbor i, inversely proportional to distance raised to θ.
nAll other centroids used as neighbors — IDW is a global method, not k-nearest.

How θ and every model in the table were scored: LOOCV

Leave-one-out cross-validation removes one of the 140 neighborhoods, predicts its value from the other 139, and records the error. Repeating this for every neighborhood — once per model, once per candidate θ — gives an honest estimate of predictive accuracy without a separate holdout set, which this dataset is too small to afford.

1

Hold out one point

Remove neighborhood i from the 140 centroids.

2

Fit on the rest

Compute weights (IDW) or a variogram (kriging) using the remaining 139.

3

Predict the held-out point

Estimate log(theft_count+1) at neighborhood i's location.

4

Repeat ×140, score

Average the squared and absolute errors into RMSE and MAE.

4 Full Model Comparison

All 9 candidate models, RMSE and MAE

The deck's ranking chart shows RMSE only. The table below adds MAE (mean absolute error, less sensitive to outlier neighborhoods than RMSE) and groups models by family. Both metrics agree on the ranking: Kriging (Spherical) and IDW (θ=3) are effectively tied at the top.

Rank Model Family LOOCV RMSE LOOCV MAE

Why IDW (θ=3), not Kriging (Spherical), was chosen for the deck: the RMSE gap between them (0.685 vs. 0.690) is under 1% — not a meaningful difference given only 140 fitting points. IDW needs no variogram to fit on data with repeated coordinates (multiple incidents share a neighborhood centroid), has one parameter instead of three (nugget, sill, range), and both metrics — RMSE and MAE — agree on the same near-tie. Kriging (Gaussian) is included here for completeness; it is numerically the least stable of the three variogram families on this dataset and is the one most likely to fail to converge depending on the fitting starting values, which is why it was left out of the deck's headline chart.

5 Limitations

Where the uncertainty estimate is a proxy, not a guarantee

The deck's uncertainty map is real and useful, but the method behind it has real limitations worth stating plainly.

IDW has no native variance estimator

Unlike kriging, which derives prediction variance directly from the fitted variogram, IDW is a deterministic weighted average with no built-in notion of confidence. Any "uncertainty" shown for an IDW surface has to be approximated from somewhere else.

The approximation used: interpolated LOOCV squared error

The deck's uncertainty map interpolates the squared LOOCV errors observed at the 140 training centroids across the same grid used for the risk surface. This is a reasonable proxy — it's genuinely low where the model was accurate nearby and high where it wasn't — but it is a backward-looking measure of past cross-validation error, not a forward-looking predictive interval with a formal coverage guarantee (e.g. "90% of true values fall in this band").

It inherits the sparsity of the training data

At the data-sparse municipal boundary, LOOCV errors are themselves estimated from fewer nearby points, so the "high uncertainty" reading there is partly a statement about data scarcity, not just model disagreement.

Better alternatives exist, at a cost

Bootstrap resampling of the 140 centroids, or conditional simulation (standard in geostatistics for kriging), would produce calibrated predictive intervals. Both were out of scope here given the small sample size (140 points) and project timeline, but are the natural next step for anyone extending this analysis.
